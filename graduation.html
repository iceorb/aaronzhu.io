graduation

Oct 14, 2026 · Working essay · 3 min read

I moved to New York a year ago.

The closest analogy I might give the city is college without an end date, only with more money and considerably more high-end alcohol.

Everyone lives in a cramped apartment with pay-per-load laundry and (potentially) horrific roommates.

People fall in love, fall out, and find someone new within a few days. Friend groups form, fracture, and reassemble. Every weekend feels like another orientation week.

The question that perplexes me, then, is the same as it was in college: how do you decide what’s important?

The most legible answer is money.

Money is easily quantifiable and readily translates into nicer apartments, Michelin stars, and bottles at the club. But New York has a way of convincing you that there’s always something else worth pursuing. There’s always someone making more than you, someone more attractive, someone more interesting.

In other cities, the answer to what matters feels more readily prescribed. In SF, you should be raising your next round. In LA, cultivating your looks and proximity to stardom. In the Midwest, perhaps settling down, buying a house, and starting a family.

These are caricatures, of course, but they offer something reassuring: a direction.

New York offers all of these ambitions at once. You can spend Monday obsessing over your career, Wednesday wondering whether you should become an artist, and Friday deciding what you really need is to fall in love.

The tragic irony is that having more choices doesn’t necessarily make choosing easier. When every life seems available to you, committing to one feels like sacrificing all the others.

In college, this indecision was practically a virtue. You were supposed to experiment, meet people, change your mind, and reinvent yourself every semester. Eventually, graduation would force your hand.

New York offers no such graduation.
You can be twenty-three at thirty-three, only with a nicer apartment and a more expensive drinking habit. You can cycle through jobs, relationships, and identities without ever committing to one.

And I have to admit that I like this about New York.

I like that I can change my mind. That nothing feels particularly permanent. That there are still a hundred different lives I could conceivably live.

In fact, I suspect that's part of why I moved here in the first place.

But lately I've been wondering whether the freedom to become anyone is making me reluctant to actually become someone.

It's easy to convince myself that every new job, relationship, or apartment constitutes progress. And sometimes it does. But progress toward what?

I don't think growing up necessarily means settling down, or abandoning the things that make New York exciting. I think it means deciding that something matters enough to give up other possibilities for it.

And that's a much harder decision than I would like to admit.

Perhaps the peculiar trap of New York is that it lets you postpone growing up indefinitely, while making that postponement feel like growth.

College ended whether we were ready or not.

In New York, you have to decide when to graduate.

I'm not entirely sure I want to yet.
